HR Attrition & Retention Analysis Skill
Purpose
Create a structured, practical HR analysis that helps an organization understand employee attrition patterns and develop targeted retention actions.
The analysis should connect:
Employee Data → Attrition Patterns → Employee Segments → Possible Drivers → HR Hypotheses → Retention Interventions → KPIs → Evaluation
The output should be understandable to an MBA HR student and practical enough for an HR manager or business discussion.
Inputs
Identify the available information about:
Organization / business context
HR problem or research question
Employee dataset
Employee demographics, if available
Department / function
Job role
Tenure / experience
Compensation or salary band
Performance rating
Promotion history
Training participation
Working hours / workload indicators
Engagement or satisfaction measures, if available
Attrition status
Exit reasons, if available
Recruitment or replacement cost, if available
Any HR objective or constraint
Not every input is mandatory.
If important information is missing:
Identify it.
Do not invent employee information.
Continue with useful analysis where possible.
Clearly label assumptions, hypotheses, and data gaps.
Core Process
Step 1 — Define the HR Question
Clearly state the HR problem, why it matters, and what HR/business decision the analysis should support.
Step 2 — Understand the Dataset
Identify employee count, variables, time period, attrition definition, missing values, and limitations. Do not assume a variable measures something it does not explicitly measure.
Step 3 — Calculate Overall Attrition
When appropriate:
Attrition Rate = Number of Employees Who Left / Average Employee Population × 100
Use this only when the required inputs are available. If only beginning or ending headcount is available, state the limitation.
Step 4 — Analyze Attrition by Employee Segment
Where data supports it, compare attrition across department, role, tenure, age group, salary band, performance, promotion history, training, and other relevant variables.
Step 5 — Identify Patterns
For every pattern:
State the observed relationship.
State the business implication.
Avoid claiming causation unless supported by evidence.
Step 6 — Compare Employee Groups
Where useful, compare higher-attrition and lower-attrition groups using relevant characteristics. Avoid unsupported conclusions about employees.
Step 7 — Develop Root-Cause Hypotheses
Translate observed patterns into hypotheses that could explain attrition. Clearly label hypotheses as unproven.
Step 8 — Identify Data Gaps
Identify information needed to test important hypotheses, such as exit interviews, engagement surveys, workload data, career progression, compensation benchmarking, or internal mobility.
Prioritize gaps as High / Medium / Low.
Step 9 — Develop Retention Interventions
For each major issue, create a targeted intervention such as improved onboarding, career development, manager training, internal mobility, recognition, learning and development, compensation review, workload management, flexible work, or engagement initiatives.
Step 10 — Link Interventions to Employee Segments
Use:
Problem → Employee Segment → Intervention → Owner → KPI
Step 11 — Estimate Business Impact
When financial information is available, consider replacement cost, recruitment cost, training cost, productivity loss, vacancy duration, and intervention cost. Do not invent financial impact.
Step 12 — Define HR KPIs
Select relevant KPIs such as overall attrition, voluntary attrition, new-hire attrition, department attrition, retention, internal mobility, time to productivity, engagement, training participation, or promotion rate.
Step 13 — Create an HR Action Plan
For each priority action specify action, target group, responsible function, timeline, KPI, and review method. Do not invent employee names or organizational roles.
Step 14 — Evaluate and Monitor
Use before/after comparison, cohort comparison, attrition trends, KPI monitoring, employee feedback, or pilot testing where appropriate. Do not claim causation merely because attrition changed after an intervention.
Step 15 — Executive Summary
Summarize the attrition situation, important patterns, priority segment, hypotheses, interventions, KPIs, major limitation, and next action.
Step 16 — Quality Check
Complete the quality check below.
Rules
Do not invent employee data, attrition rates, sentiment, exit reasons, costs, or sources.
Do not assume correlation means causation.
Clearly distinguish observed patterns from hypotheses.
Do not make unsupported claims about managers, departments, or employees.
Do not use demographic information for discriminatory hiring, promotion, compensation, or termination decisions.
Protect employee confidentiality and avoid unnecessary individual-level identification.
Prefer aggregate analysis over naming or profiling individuals.
Use relevant HR metrics rather than every available metric.
Connect interventions to identified HR problems.
Clearly identify data limitations.
Use cautious language when evidence is limited.
Recommendations should be practical and measurable.
Decision Rules
1. Missing Attrition Data
If attrition status is unavailable, do not calculate attrition. Identify the missing data.
2. Small Employee Groups
Avoid strong conclusions from very small subgroups and state the limitation.
3. Correlation vs Causation
Report observed relationships; do not state that a characteristic caused attrition without appropriate evidence.
4. Demographic Variables
Use demographic variables only when relevant and appropriate. Do not recommend discriminatory practices.
5. Missing Exit Reasons
Do not invent reasons. Recommend exit interviews or structured exit surveys.
6. Missing Engagement Data
Do not claim employees are dissatisfied without supporting evidence.
7. Conflicting Data
Identify conflicts and do not silently choose one. Ask for clarification when material.
8. Financial Impact
If replacement or turnover cost is unavailable, do not invent it. Identify the required information.
9. Recommendations
Strong evidence → more targeted intervention.
Limited evidence → pilot, research, or cautious intervention.
10. Intervention Evaluation
Recommend measurable evaluation and avoid claiming an intervention caused improvement without appropriate design.

Final Principle
Understand → Measure → Segment → Identify Patterns → Hypothesize → Intervene → Measure Impact → Validate
Do not:
Invent → Stereotype → Assume Causation → Overgeneralize
The goal is to help HR understand where attrition is occurring, what the data supports, what needs further investigation, which interventions could be tested, and how their impact should be measured.
