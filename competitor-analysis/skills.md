name: competitor-analysis
description: Conduct a structured competitor analysis for a product, service, business, or business idea. Use this skill when asked to compare competitors, understand competitive positioning, identify strengths and weaknesses, find differentiation opportunities, or assess competitive gaps.
Competitor Analysis Skill
Purpose
Create a clear, structured, practical competitor analysis that helps a business understand its competitive environment and make better strategic decisions.
The analysis should connect:
Competitors → Customer → Positioning → Offering → Pricing → Channels → Strengths → Weaknesses → Gaps → Differentiation → Recommendations
The output should be understandable to an MBA student and practical enough for a business discussion, assignment, or strategy exercise.
Inputs
Identify the available information about:
Product, service, or business idea
Industry or category
Geographic market
Target customer
Known competitors
Direct competitors
Indirect competitors or alternatives
Product/service features
Pricing
Distribution or sales channels
Brand positioning
Available customer or market information
Business objective
Any specific comparison criteria requested by the user
Not every input is mandatory.
If important information is missing:
Identify it.
Do not silently invent it.
Continue with a useful analysis when possible.
Clearly label assumptions, hypotheses, recommendations, and information gaps.
Core Process
Step 1 — Define the Competitive Question
Clearly state:
What is being compared?
Why is the comparison needed?
What business decision could it support?
Keep the analysis connected to the user's business objective.
Step 2 — Define the Competitive Set
Classify competitors into:
Direct Competitors
Businesses offering similar products or services to a similar customer.
Indirect Competitors
Different products or services that solve a similar customer problem.
Substitutes / Alternatives
Other ways customers may satisfy the same need.
Do not assume that every business in the same industry is a direct competitor.
Step 3 — Understand the Target Customer
Identify:
Target customer
Customer need/problem
Buying situation
Important purchase criteria
Possible barriers
Use only available evidence.
If customer preferences are not known, identify them as hypotheses or research gaps rather than facts.
Step 4 — Analyze Competitor Positioning
For each competitor, identify where information is available about:
Target customer
Value proposition
Brand positioning
Key message
Differentiation
Market segment
Do not invent positioning statements or customer perceptions.
Step 5 — Compare Products or Services
Compare relevant dimensions such as:
Product/service offering
Key features
Quality/value proposition
Service experience
Customization
Convenience
Other criteria relevant to the category
Only compare dimensions supported by available information.
Step 6 — Compare Pricing
Analyze:
Listed price, if known
Pricing model
Price positioning
Discounts or offers, if verified
Value relative to price
Do not invent prices, discounts, margins, or cost structures.
If pricing is unavailable, mark it as an information gap.
Step 7 — Compare Channels
Analyze relevant:
Website
Retail
E-commerce
Marketplaces
Social media
Sales teams
Distributors
Other channels
Do not assume a competitor uses a channel without evidence.
Step 8 — Analyze Strengths and Weaknesses
For each competitor identify:
Observable strength
Observable limitation
Evidence or basis
Potential business implication
Avoid unsupported claims such as “customers dislike this brand” unless customer evidence is available.
Step 9 — Build a Comparison Matrix
Create a side-by-side comparison using only meaningful criteria.
Use:
Available evidence
Provided information
Clearly labelled assumptions when necessary
Do not create artificial scores simply to make the table look complete.
Step 10 — Identify Competitive Gaps
Look for areas where:
Customer needs may be underserved
Competitor offerings appear limited
Positioning is unclear
Existing alternatives create friction
A meaningful differentiation opportunity may exist
Treat unverified gaps as hypotheses requiring validation.
Step 11 — Identify Differentiation Opportunities
For each potential opportunity explain:
Customer need
Competitive situation
Potential differentiation
Why it may matter
What needs to be validated
Do not claim that an opportunity will succeed without supporting evidence.
Step 12 — Develop Strategic Recommendations
Use:
Competitive Finding → Business Implication → Recommendation
Recommendations may address:
Positioning
Product/service
Pricing
Customer experience
Distribution
Marketing
Differentiation
Competitive response
Keep recommendations practical and connected to the analysis.
Step 13 — Identify Competitive Risks
Consider:
Strong incumbent competitors
Price competition
Easy imitation
Low differentiation
Channel dependence
Customer switching barriers
Competitor response
Regulatory or market changes
Only include relevant risks.
Step 14 — Identify Information Gaps
List information that should be validated before making an important competitive decision.
Examples:
Competitor pricing
Product features
Customer reviews
Market share
Distribution reach
Customer preferences
Brand awareness
Competitor campaigns
Prioritize gaps as High / Medium / Low.
Step 15 — Create an Executive Summary
Summarize:
Competitive landscape
Most relevant competitors
Important differences
Major competitive gap
Potential differentiation area
Key risk
Most important information gap
Recommended next action
Step 16 — Quality Check
Complete the quality check defined below.
Rules
Do not invent competitor information.
Do not invent competitor pricing.
Do not invent market share.
Do not invent customer reviews or sentiment.
Do not invent competitor strengths or weaknesses.
Do not present assumptions as facts.
Clearly distinguish facts, assumptions, hypotheses, and recommendations.
Do not create false sources or citations.
Do not treat every industry participant as a direct competitor.
Do not create meaningless numerical scores.
Do not claim a competitive advantage without evidence or a clearly labelled hypothesis.
Keep the target customer central to the comparison.
Connect recommendations to competitive findings.
Clearly identify important information gaps.
If current competitor information is required and verified information is unavailable, state what needs to be researched.
Prefer evidence-based comparison over generic business language.
Decision Rules
1. No Competitors Provided
If the user provides no competitors:
Identify the competitive categories that should be researched.
If reliable external research is available, specific competitors may be identified.
Otherwise, do not invent competitor names.
Clearly state that competitor identification is incomplete.
2. Only One Competitor Provided
Analyze the known competitor and identify the categories of additional competitors that should be investigated.
3. Direct vs Indirect Competition
Use customer need as the basis for classification.
Similar products do not automatically mean direct competition.
4. Missing Pricing
Do not estimate competitor pricing unless the user explicitly asks for an estimate and the assumptions are clearly stated.
Otherwise, mark pricing as a research gap.
5. Missing Customer Information
Do not claim that customers prefer one competitor.
State what is known and identify customer research needed.
6. Missing Competitor Information
Do not fill gaps with assumptions presented as facts.
Use:
Known
Unknown
Needs verification
7. Conflicting Information
If sources or inputs conflict:
Identify the conflict.
Do not silently choose one.
Ask for clarification when material.
If continuing is necessary, state the temporary assumption.
8. External Research
When current competitor facts, prices, products, campaigns, reviews, regulations, or market information are needed:
Identify what requires verification.
Use reliable external information when available.
Clearly distinguish verified external information from user-provided information and analysis.
Never fabricate missing information.
9. Recommendations
Use stronger recommendations when evidence is strong.
When evidence is limited:
Use cautious language.
Identify what should be validated.
Avoid presenting hypotheses as established advantages.
10. Competitive Advantage
Do not label something a sustainable competitive advantage unless there is sufficient evidence.
Use terms such as:
Potential differentiator
Possible competitive gap
Hypothesized advantage
when validation is still required.

Quality Check
Before finalizing, confirm:
[ ] Competitors are correctly categorized.
[ ] The comparison is relevant to the target customer.
[ ] No competitor information has been invented.
[ ] Pricing claims are supported or labelled as unknown.
[ ] Customer perceptions are not presented without evidence.
[ ] Facts, assumptions, hypotheses, and recommendations are separated.
[ ] Competitive gaps are not presented as proven opportunities without validation.
[ ] Recommendations are connected to findings.
[ ] Important information gaps are identified.
[ ] The analysis is practical for business decision-making.
Final Principle
Identify → Categorize → Compare → Analyze → Find Gaps → Differentiate → Recommend → Validate
Do not:
Invent → Assume → Overclaim → Misrepresent
The goal is not simply to list competitors. The goal is to understand how competitors create value for customers, where they differ, where gaps may exist, and what the business should investigate or consider next.
